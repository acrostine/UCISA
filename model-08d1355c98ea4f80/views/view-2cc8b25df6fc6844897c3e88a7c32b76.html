Enterprise Architecture ()
## Purpose Enterprise Architecture establishes and maintains a holistic view of the institution's business capabilities, information systems, and technology infrastructure. It translates strategic objectives into architectural blueprints and roadmaps, ensuring that technology investments and capability changes are aligned with academic and operational goals. This process enables informed decision-making, reduces redundancy, and supports institutional agility. ## Roles - Enterprise Architect: Leads the development of architecture models and roadmaps, facilitates stakeholder reviews, and ensures alignment with standards. - Domain Architect (Business/Data/Application/Technology): Provides domain-specific expertise and input for architecture models. - Chief Information Officer (CIO): Approves major architecture decisions and roadmaps. - Stakeholder (Academic/Operational Leadership): Provides strategic context and validates business capability priorities. - System: Automated tools for model repository, diagramming, and standards library. ## Upstream Inputs - Strategy Documents (e.g., strategic plan, academic vision) - Technology Standards (e.g., security policies, platform standards) - Operational Data (e.g., system inventories, capability heatmaps) - Business Capability Model (current state) - Stakeholder Requirements and Priorities ## Downstream Outputs - Current-State Architecture Model - Future-State Architecture Model - Architecture Roadmap (phased transition plan) - Gap Analysis Report - Architecture Decision Record (ADR) - Approved Architecture Blueprint ## Key Decisions - What is the scope and level of detail for the architecture model (enterprise vs. segment)? - Which gaps between current and future state require immediate action vs. deferred? - Which technology standards or patterns should be mandated vs. recommended? - What is the priority sequence of roadmap initiatives? - Does the proposed architecture adequately address strategic objectives? ## Exception Paths - If stakeholder input is insufficient or conflicting, the Enterprise Architect may escalate to the CIO for resolution. - If a gap is deemed too large or risky to address in the current planning cycle, it is logged in a risk register and deferred. - If the architecture model fails review, it is returned for rework with specific findings. - If technology standards are outdated or missing, a standards update request is initiated as a parallel activity. ## Step Walkthrough 1. Gather Strategic Inputs: The Enterprise Architect initiates the process by gathering all relevant inputs. This includes the latest strategic plan, academic vision documents, approved technology standards, security policies, and operational data such as system inventories and capability heatmaps. Inputs are sourced from stakeholders, document repositories, and automated data feeds. All inputs are catalogued and validated for completeness. 2. Assess Current State: The Enterprise Architect examines the current business capability model, system inventories, and technology landscape. This step produces a detailed current-state architecture model that captures as-is capabilities, applications, data flows, and infrastructure components. The assessment identifies pain points, redundancies, and areas of technical debt. 3. Define Target State: Working with domain architects and stakeholders, the Enterprise Architect defines the target architecture. This includes future business capabilities, information systems, data architecture, and technology infrastructure. The target state is designed to meet strategic goals, improve agility, and comply with technology standards. Multiple options may be considered and documented. 4. Perform Gap Analysis: The Enterprise Architect systematically compares the current-state and future-state models. Gaps are identified in capabilities, applications, data, and technology. Each gap is assessed for impact, risk, and effort. The output is a Gap Analysis Report that prioritizes gaps and recommends actions such as retire, consolidate, enhance, or build new. 5. Review Architecture Model: The Enterprise Architect facilitates a review session with key stakeholders, domain architects, and the CIO. The current-state model, future-state model, and Gap Analysis Report are presented. Stakeholders provide feedback on alignment with strategic objectives, completeness, and feasibility. The CIO makes the final decision to approve or request changes. 6. Resolve Review Findings: If the review identifies issues or requested changes, the Enterprise Architect updates the current-state model, future-state model, or gap analysis accordingly. Each finding is tracked and resolved. The updated models are then resubmitted for re-review. This step may loop multiple times until approval is obtained. 7. Develop Roadmap: Upon approval of the architecture models, the Enterprise Architect develops a multi-phase roadmap. The roadmap sequences initiatives based on priority, dependencies, resource availability, and strategic impact. Each phase includes specific milestones, deliverables, and responsible parties. The roadmap is designed to guide incremental capability evolution. 8. Approve Roadmap: The Enterprise Architect presents the Architecture Roadmap to the CIO for final approval. The CIO evaluates the roadmap for strategic alignment, feasibility, and resource implications. Upon approval, the roadmap is published and becomes the authoritative guide for architecture-related decisions and investments.
